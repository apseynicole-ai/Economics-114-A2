Big map / what this unit is actually doing

Stage 1 · Dark Purple

The core job of Unit 2

Unit 2 takes the supply-side model from Unit 1 and adds distribution. It asks not only how many people are employed and what real wage is set in equilibrium, but also who gets what share of output. The key bridge is the Lorenz curve: the WS–PS model can be translated into an inequality picture.

Master question
Why do countries that look similar—especially Spain, Germany, and Denmark—produce such different combinations of unemployment, real wage growth, and inequality?

Main unit route

  • 2.1 sets up the puzzle with Mar in Spain and Germany.
  • 2.2 introduces Lorenz curves, Gini coefficients, and market vs disposable income.
  • 2.3 maps WS–PS equilibrium into a Lorenz curve.
  • 2.4–2.8 show how policies and shocks shift WS and PS.
  • 2.9–2.11 apply the model to the US, Denmark, Germany, and Spain.
  • 2.12 explains why the supply-side model is not enough on its own.

Quick recall / before you go deep

Stage 2 · Pastel Purple

Definitions you must know cold

  • Gini coefficient: numerical measure of inequality from 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (extreme inequality).
  • Lorenz curve: cumulative share of income/wealth plotted against cumulative share of population from poorest to richest.
  • Market income: income before tax and transfers.
  • Disposable income: income after tax and transfers.
  • Structural unemployment: unemployment at the WS–PS Nash equilibrium.
  • Markup / profit share: higher markup means a lower wage share and a lower PS curve.
  • Primary labour market: higher wages, more security, often unionized.
  • Secondary labour market: lower wages, lower security, precarious contracts.

Fast logic rules

  • PS up → higher real wage + more employment.
  • PS down → lower real wage + less employment.
  • WS up on its own → higher structural unemployment.
  • Higher productivity with unchanged shares → PS up.
  • Higher taxes or imported-input costs → PS down.
  • Higher markup / less competition → PS down + more inequality.
  • More unemployment and/or lower wage share → Lorenz curve bows further from equality line.

Inequality tools: Lorenz curve, Gini, and disposable income

Stage 3 · Dark Blue

How inequality is measured

The Gini coefficient is a summary number, but the Lorenz curve gives the full shape of inequality. The more bowed the Lorenz curve is away from the 45° equality line, the more unequal the distribution. The area method gives:

Gini = A / (A + B)

That is why the landowners example matters: if the first 90% of the population owns nothing, the curve lies flat for most of the horizontal axis and then rises sharply at the end. That is extreme inequality.

Market income vs disposable income

Unit 2 does not stop with wages before government intervention. Disposable income is what households can actually spend after taxes and transfers. Government redistribution can pull the Lorenz curve closer to the equality line even if market income is very unequal.

Exam insight
If the question says “living standards”, disposable income is often the more meaningful comparison. If the question says “who earns what before redistribution”, use market income.

WS–PS + Lorenz curve: how equilibrium becomes inequality

Stage 4 · Light Blue

The key bridge in this unit

In the WS–PS model, equilibrium determines three things together: the real wage, employment/unemployment, and the division of output between wage income and profit income. Because of that, one supply-side equilibrium can be translated directly into a Lorenz curve.

In the textbook example, a population of 100 has 10 owners, 80 employed workers, and 10 unemployed workers. If the employed workers receive 60% of total income as wages and the owners receive 40%, then the Lorenz curve has:

  • a flat first segment because unemployed workers receive no income,
  • a middle upward segment because employed workers all earn the same wage,
  • a final steep segment because owners take the remaining profit share.

That is why more unemployment or a lower wage share increases inequality.

wage share = w / λ = 1 − σ

So if competition increases and markup falls, the PS curve shifts up, the wage share rises, unemployment falls, and the Lorenz curve moves closer to equality.

Labour market policies: intended gains + unintended consequences

Stage 5 · Dark Green

Education and training

If education and training raise labour productivity λ, and the markup is unchanged, the PS curve shifts up. Workers get a higher real wage, firms employ more labour, and inequality falls because unemployment falls while the profit share is unchanged.

Wage subsidy

A wage subsidy lowers firms’ effective labour cost. Because the markup is unchanged, firms set a lower price, the real wage rises, and structural unemployment falls. The mechanism is similar to higher productivity: lower unit cost pushes PS up.

Unemployment benefits

This is the big warning example. In the short run, higher unemployment benefits raise the reservation option of workers, so the WS curve shifts up and both unemployed people and employed workers look better off. But in the long run, the new Nash equilibrium can have higher structural unemployment. The unemployed are better off, but fewer people have jobs.

Very testable contrast
Education/training and wage subsidies push PS up. Unemployment benefits, by themselves, push WS up. Those are very different policy stories.

Why Sweden is special in the model

At first glance, generous unemployment benefits should raise structural unemployment. Sweden’s solidaristic package shows why outcomes can differ if other institutions move too. The three linked policies were:

  • Equal wages for equal work, negotiated nationally.
  • Generous but relatively short-term unemployment benefits.
  • Active labour market policy such as retraining and mobility support.

The result is that low-productivity firms exit, higher-productivity firms expand, average productivity rises, and the PS curve shifts up enough to offset the upward WS pressure from unemployment benefits. This creates a better equilibrium than “benefits alone” would predict.

The data warning is important: generous benefits do not automatically imply high unemployment. Institutional packages matter.

Labour unions + segmented labour markets

Stage 6 · Pastel Green

Unions can push WS up — but that is not the whole story

If unions bargain for wages above the minimum necessary to prevent shirking, the bargaining curve lies above the original WS curve. In the simplest version, that means lower employment and unchanged equilibrium real wages in the long run.

But the unit then adds two correctives:

  • Union restraint: powerful unions may choose not to fully use bargaining power because they understand general-equilibrium effects on jobs.
  • Union voice effect: cooperative relations can lower the disutility of effort, shift WS down, raise productivity, or even shift PS up.

That is why union coverage and unemployment do not show a simple positive relationship across countries.

High-level rule
In this unit, unions are not “always good” or “always bad”. Their net effect depends on bargaining pressure, restraint, voice, productivity effects, and the surrounding institutions.

US example: weaker unions and more inequality

The textbook gives the US as a time-series example: union density fell sharply from the early 1980s, while earnings inequality rose. In model terms, weaker unions and weaker worker bargaining power contribute to a downward shift in the WS curve. That can help explain why unemployment did not rise as much as a falling-competition story alone would predict.

Segmented labour markets

Unit 2 then separates the labour market into a primary segment and a secondary segment. Carmen belongs to the primary labour market: better wages, stronger security, promotion ladders, often unionized. Mar is trapped in the secondary market: insecure contracts, lower pay, lower bargaining power. Segmentation increases inequality even when workers have similar skills.

Eliminating segmentation raises low wages, lowers high wages somewhat, and moves the Lorenz curve closer to equality. The product-market side is unchanged, so the PS curve is unaffected in that example.

Taxes + imported materials: why PS can fall even without weaker worker power

Stage 7 · Pastel Yellow

Taxes in the WS–PS model

Once taxes are introduced, we care about the post-tax real consumption wage. The WS curve stays written in terms of the real wage workers can actually consume, but the PS curve changes because taxes reduce the output available to be shared between workers and firms.

Pc = P(1 + tv) Wgross = W(1 + td) w = W / Pc PS with tax: w = ((1 − σ)λ) / ((1 + td)(1 + tv))

So a higher direct tax or consumption tax shifts PS down. Real wages fall and structural unemployment rises, other things equal. If education/training is financed by tax, the tax effect partly offsets the productivity effect.

Imported materials

If firms rely on imported raw materials or energy, higher import costs raise marginal cost. Then workers and firms are not the only claimants on output anymore: foreign input suppliers also take a slice.

PS with imported materials: w = ((1 − σ)λ) / (1 + ψ)

When ψ rises—think oil and gas shock—the PS curve shifts down. Real wages fall and structural unemployment rises. This is one reason imported-energy shocks matter so much for macro outcomes.

Application: did falling competition increase inequality in the US?

Stage 8 · Light Orange

The model’s US story

Governments use competition policy mostly as a micro tool, but Unit 2 shows a macro damage from excessive market power too. Less competition means a higher markup. In the WS–PS model, that is a lower PS curve, which means a lower wage share, a higher profit share, and—if nothing else changes—higher structural unemployment.

The textbook then shows three US trends moving in the same broad direction over time:

  • average markup rises,
  • profit share rises,
  • market-income Gini rises.

But the model initially predicts more unemployment too, which did not happen clearly in the US. So the textbook adds a second force: weaker unions / weaker worker bargaining power shifted WS down. The combined movement can produce higher inequality without a rise in unemployment.

Important nuance
The textbook also flags automation and the China shock as additional reasons inequality rose in the US. So competition is a major explanation in the unit, but not the only one.

How the model resolves the puzzle

If only PS falls because markup rises, the economy moves from A to B: lower wage share and more unemployment. But if WS also shifts down because worker bargaining weakens, the final equilibrium can move to C: inequality still rises, yet unemployment need not rise and may even fall somewhat.

So the US application is really a combined product-market and labour-market power story.

Denmark flexicurity + Germany vs Spain

Stage 9 · Darker Orange

Denmark’s flexicurity triangle

Denmark combines employer flexibility with worker security. Employers can hire and fire relatively easily, but workers receive income support for a limited period and strong active labour market policies like retraining and job matching. The model suggests several possible effects:

  • higher productivity can shift PS up,
  • cooperative labour relations can shift WS down,
  • easy firing can also shift WS down because shirking becomes riskier,
  • but generous benefits can shift WS up because life without a job improves.

The overall Danish outcome is consistent with a successful institutional package, not a single policy lever.

Germany vs Spain

Germany performs better because its system combines stronger worker–employer cooperation, higher labour productivity, and more competitive product markets. Spain suffers from slower productivity growth, weaker firm-level cooperation, more difficult firing rules, and higher markups. That means Germany tends to have a higher PS and a lower WS than Spain.

How good is the model? + exam traps

Stage 10 · Red

What the model misses

The WS–PS framework is powerful, but Unit 2 explicitly says it is incomplete. First, the equilibrium can act like a weak magnet: adjustment can be slow. German reunification shows this. East German unemployment jumped and stayed high for a long time because wages rose toward West German levels and East German firms could not compete. If the model adjusted quickly, that hump would have disappeared much faster.

Second, the model is supply-side only. It cannot explain why economists and governments devote so much effort to stabilizing demand shocks. That is why Unit 3 introduces the demand side and aggregate demand.

What to write in a paragraph answer
“The WS–PS model is useful for showing how productivity, competition, taxation, benefits, unions, and input costs affect structural unemployment, wages, and inequality. But it does not fully explain slow adjustment or macroeconomic slumps caused by weak demand, which is why the demand side is introduced in Unit 3.”

Mistakes to avoid

  • Confusing market income with disposable income.
  • Writing that a higher unemployment benefit automatically raises unemployment in the data.
  • Saying unions always raise unemployment.
  • Forgetting that higher markup = lower PS = lower wage share.
  • Thinking taxes shift WS instead of PS in this setup.
  • Forgetting that imported materials create a claim on output by foreign suppliers.
  • Treating Denmark’s outcome as “because benefits are generous” without mentioning the whole flexicurity package.
